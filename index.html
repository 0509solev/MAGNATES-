it is gentle.

The Philippines I would design

The Philippines I would design is strong before it is gentle. I aim to describe a state that

treats its own survival and prosperity as the first duty of government,

asks for service from every citizen before it asks for their opinions,

and behaves, in a way most modern democracies would find faintly embarrassing, like a fortress that never quite believes the siege is over.

What follows goes through the design in order.

  1. The ground rules of the exercise

  2. The temperament I am borrowing from Lee Kuan Yew

  3. The form of government that temperament requires

  4. The figure in our own history who came closest to it

  5. The institutions I would import: where each comes from, what the evidence says about it, and what would have to change here for it to work

One ground rule needs stating early, because it changes what counts as an objection.

This is my ideal Philippines, so I assume that every condition is ideal.

  • The 1987 Constitution has been amended through the process set out in its own Article XVII.
  • The political class has consented instead of resisted.
  • The person at the head of government cannot be bought.
  • Every borrowed institution works here the way it works where it came from.

(Republic of the Philippines, 1987)

We would tend to think this makes the exercise easy, since obstacles are simply waved away. It does something more useful.

Execution risk Design risk

It strips out execution risk and leaves design risk standing, so any cost that still appears belongs to the model itself and not to bad luck.

Ideal, in this sense, does not mean nice.

It means the machine runs as drawn.

At a People’s Action Party rally at Fullerton Square in 1980, describing how he had ended unrest at Singapore Airlines, Lee Kuan Yew said that whoever governs Singapore must have

“that iron in him,”

or give it up.

(Channel NewsAsia, n.d.; Channel NewsAsia, 2023)

It is tempting to hear machismo in that, a strongman enjoying the sound of his own voice.

However, the sentence is doing the work of an argument.

The person governing has to

absorb unpopularity,

refuse to be talked out of a hard decision,

and stay clean while doing it.

If that sounds less like a personality and more like a job specification, that is because it is one.

Dismissing the line as swagger would be to miss the specification inside it.

Lee gave the policy version to the Philippine Business Conference in 1992, when he said

a country needs discipline more than democracy to develop,

(The Philippine Star, 2015)

and that after debate the legislature must let the executive take the hard decisions.

(Lee, 1992)

That argument fixes the form of government.

The presidential system the Philippines inherited from the Americans splits authority between a president and a Congress that can each obstruct the other.

Lee warned in 1992 that there was no assurance such a constitution would produce wealth here as it had in the United States.

(Lee, 1992)

By his account, even President Ramos agreed in private that British-style parliamentary constitutions work better

because the majority party in the legislature is also the government,

although in public he had to say otherwise.

(The Philippine Star, 2015)

So the ideal Philippines is a

dominant-party, executive-led parliamentary state on the Singaporean model,

drawing on the Westminster tradition Singapore inherited.

The party that wins the mandate governs, and the deadlock that presidential systems treat as a feature is designed out.

One Singaporean touch I would keep is an elected head of state, separate from the Prime Minister, whose concurrence is needed for key appointments.

Its use will become clear shortly.

The amendments to Articles VI and VII that all this requires are among the conditions I stipulate.

(Chan, 2023)

We would expect a Filipino essay on strongman government to be about Ferdinand Marcos Sr., and this one cannot avoid him,

because he is the closest thing this country produced to a Lee Kuan Yew,

in method if not in result.

The two ruled in the same era and reached for the same tools. Marcos announced martial law in September 1972 and built a New Society around a slogan Lee could almost have written:

Sa ikauunlad ng bayan, disiplina ang kailangan.

National progress requires discipline.

(Martial Law Museum, n.d.)

He gave the economy to a corps of technocrats around Cesar Virata, later his prime minister,

whom the public read, for a time, as the apolitical and comparatively clean part of the regime.

(Tadem, 2013)

≈9%

Growth followed: it approached nine percent in both 1973 and 1976.

(ABS-CBN News, 2021)

On this view, here was our Lee, undone only by bad luck and an ungrateful electorate.

That rescue proves too much.

The man best placed to judge did not accept it.

Lee wrote that only in the Philippines could a leader who

“pillaged his country”

for over twenty years be considered for a national burial,

and that Marcos let his wife and cronies strip the state through monopolies while putting the government heavily in debt.

(The Philippine Star, 2016)

The numbers side with Lee.

3.8%

average growth over the Marcos years, against peaks near nine percent.

  • The country suffered its worst recession in 1984 and 1985.
  • Debt ran above two hundred percent of exports from 1978 onward, peaking in 1985.

(ABS-CBN News, 2021)

Tadem (2016) explains why the technocrats could not stop the slide:

their network could not control Imelda Marcos’s spending or the cronies’ sugar and coconut monopolies.

There was a human bill as well.

11,103

martial law victims recognised by the state’s own claims board, out of roughly 75,000 applicants.

(Bulatlat, 2018)

I draw a different lesson from Marcos than the usual one.

He copied the visible half of the Lee model,

  • the emergency powers,
  • the slogan,
  • the planners,

and left out the invisible half,

the incorruptible centre.

My ideal Philippines is the same draft with the missing half written in.

The first import, then, is that incorruptible centre,

and it needs two donors, because one body alone teaches only half of the lesson.

From Singapore I would take the

Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau,

  • founded in 1952,
  • housed in the Prime Minister’s Office,
  • and led by a director who reports directly to the Prime Minister.

(Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau [CPIB], n.d.-a)

Putting the graft hunter under the head of government looks like asking a goalkeeper to referee his own match,

and the clever part is the fix.

Under Article 22G of Singapore’s Constitution, if a Prime Minister refuses consent for an investigation, the director can go to the elected President, whose concurrence is also needed to appoint or remove the director.

The referee, in other words, has a direct line to the stadium owner.

(Chan, 2023)

The government’s own claim is that no Prime Minister has ever blocked the Bureau,

which is the kind of assurance we should hold at arm’s length.

12 months

Still, the 2024 jailing of a former transport minister, S. Iswaran, for twelve months over gifts and obstructing justice, with the judge treating high office as an aggravating factor and not a shield, suggests the reach is real.

(The Straits Times, 2024)

Singapore scored 84 out of 100 and ranked third in the 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index, while the Philippines scored 32 and ranked 120th.

From Hong Kong I would take the other half.

1973

Peter Godber, a police chief superintendent under investigation, escaped to Britain.

1974

Hong Kong created its Independent Commission Against Corruption.

A useful reminder that the police cannot be relied on to investigate the police.

(Hong Kong SAR Government, n.d.; Independent Commission Against Corruption [ICAC], n.d.)

Its Commissioner answers directly to the Chief Executive, and its founding idea was that prosecution is only one prong of three,

Prosecution

only one prong,

Prevention

inside institutions,

Education

of the public.

(ICAC, n.d.)

Neither body works if honesty is also a sacrifice, so the package includes Singapore’s pay rule.

Ministerial salaries are pegged to

the median income of the top 1,000 citizen earners,

less40%

to reflect the ethos of political service.

(Public Service Division, 2012)

Even Singapore pays a political price for this logic.

−36%

Ministers took a pay cut of about 36 percent in 2012 to calm public anger,

15 years

and the first raise in fifteen years, announced this month, came with the prime minister acknowledging that Singaporeans scrutinise it.

(Associated Press, 2026)

The Philippine adaptation is a matter of moving powers, not inventing them.

The 1987 Constitution gives the investigative job to the Ombudsman under Article XI, so the ideal state amends that article to create a single bureau with

  • a protected director’s tenure,
  • reach over ministers and legislators,
  • a prevention and education wing on the Hong Kong model,
  • and the route to the head of state that Singapore wrote into its own constitution.

(Republic of the Philippines, 1987)

What would really have to change is who is willing to be investigated.

In the actual Philippines, dynastic politics means the investigated are also the legislators who would have to vote for the bureau, and that is the obstacle.

I stipulate that they vote for it.

The second import is the economy, and there the obvious donor is South Korea.

Park Chung-hee took power in 1961 and created the Economic Planning Board the same year,

  • headed by a deputy prime minister,
  • staffed by bureaucrats chosen for their intellectual ability,
  • with the power to allocate resources, direct credit, and formulate the national plans.

(Library of Congress, n.d.)

Support was conditional.

In the heavy industry drive of the 1970s, firms either succeeded internationally or lost favourable government backing.

(PBS, n.d.)

Korea’s GDP per capita was about 159 US dollars in 1960 and more than 36,000 by 2024.

(PBS, n.d.)

Korea also treated the board as scaffolding.

  1. 1961

    Park creates the Economic Planning Board.

  2. 1970s

    The heavy industry drive: succeed internationally or lose favourable backing.

  3. Late 1980s

    The power to allocate credit has returned to the functional ministries.

I would copy the sunset as well.

(Library of Congress, n.d.; PBS, n.d.)

In the Philippine case the firms to be disciplined are, on the usual account, the same families that finance elections,

and the ideal stipulation is that they accept conditional support as the Korean conglomerates did.

A dominant-party parliament, which does not have to buy its majority seat by seat, helps that stipulation along.

The third import is quieter and, for my purposes, probably more important:

a state that cannot hide.

Estonia’s X-Road, in operation since 2001,

lets independent public and private databases exchange data securely under a once-only principle, so citizens give the state a given piece of information one time.

Citizens can see through the state portal who has looked at their data.

1,000+ years

of working time: what the system is credited with saving every year.

(e-Estonia, n.d.-a; e-Estonia, n.d.-b)

It belongs in a strong-state design because discretion is where graft lives.

A paper state is nothing but discretion:

a stamp here,a signature there,and a fixer in between.

A logged state has fewer places to hide a favour, and it hands the integrity bureau an audit trail it would otherwise have to build by hand.

The problem is scale.

Estonia

about 1.3 million people

The Philippines

over 100 million people, spread across more than 7,000 islands

Each dot stands for 100,000 people

So the adaptation is a long and expensive build on top of the national ID, and the ideal condition is funding and connectivity that arrive on schedule.

The fourth import keeps the iron from feeling like a boot.

Lee’s Singapore demanded a great deal, and it also gave citizens something to lose.

The Housing and Development Board, founded in 1960,

54,430

flats completed by 1965.

(Chew, 2009)

≈80%

of residents, close to eighty percent, live in its housing today.

(Housing & Development Board [HDB], n.d.)

≈90%

of those households own their home, on the 2014 figures.

(Singapore Language Councils, n.d.)

The logic, as it is usually put, is that

a citizen with a mortgage on the country has reasons to keep it standing.

The Philippines already has a savings-based housing fund in Pag-IBIG, so the adaptation is scale and land:

the state must assemble land at a pace private markets never will,

which is exactly the sort of power a strong executive has and a gridlocked one does not.

I stipulate that the land is assembled fairly, which is a bigger stipulation than it sounds.

The last import faces outward.

Singapore’s Total Defence, proclaimed in 1984 and adapted from Sweden and Switzerland, rests on five pillars, with a sixth, digital, added later.

(RSIS, 2009; Ministry of Defence, Singapore [MINDEF], n.d.)

The core idea is that a small state survives only if every part of its society counts itself as part of the defence, National Service included.

Duty, in this arrangement, is the unit of belonging:

you are counted because you serve.

(MINDEF, n.d.)

An externally strong Philippines needs the same idea for a sharper reason.

July 2016

An arbitral tribunal under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea found that China’s nine-dash line had no legal basis,

and Beijing rejected the ruling, even though the Convention makes such awards final and binding.

(International Crisis Group, 2016; Permanent Court of Arbitration, 2016)

A binding award with no bailiff

is a fair picture of what law is worth when capacity does not stand behind it.

The adaptation is

  • conscription,
  • a naval and industrial base tied to the planning board,
  • and civil defence that gives every province a role.

An archipelago is still costlier to defend than an island city, and a public with good historical reasons to distrust its own military will not hand over its young cheaply.

Ideal conditions assume

civilian control holds,

and that is the assumption I would put nearest the top of the list of things worth worrying about.

These pieces are meant to lock together instead of sitting side by side.

  1. The elected head of state

    guards the integrity bureau’s independence,

  2. The dominant-party parliament

    gives the Prime Minister the mandate to act,

  3. The pay rule

    makes honesty affordable,

  4. The digital layer

    makes concealment expensive,

  5. The planning board

    turns discipline into growth,

  6. Housing

    gives citizens a stake in the outcome,

  7. Total Defence

    turns that stake into a willingness to defend it.

Each institution answers a failure the previous one leaves open.

  1. Concentrated power is dangerous unless it can be audited,

    so the audit lives inside the state with a route around the ruler.

  2. Audits are cheap only if records exist,

    so the state logs itself.

  3. Strength is wasted without growth, and growth without a stake is resented,

    so the state builds homes.

The whole is one argument for durability:

a country that expects to be tested and prepares accordingly.

Now the honest costs, which is where assuming ideal conditions earns its keep.

With corruption and sabotage stipulated away, the objections that remain belong to the design, and I count three.

  1. Informational.
  2. The base rate.
  3. The human cost of discipline.

The first is informational.

A state that wins every argument with its critics loses its feedback.

Lee’s own view was that wrong ideas have to be challenged before they shape public opinion, which is why he answered critics sharply. That works if the executive is right.

An ideal ruler can still be wrong, and a state built to stop dissent has stopped the one signal that would say so.

(Lee, 2000)

The second is the base rate, which is unkind.

So Singapore is an exception we admire and not a rule we can expect, and the ideal condition is doing enormous work by assuming we would be the exception too.

(The Straits Times, 2025)

The third is the human cost of discipline.

The New Society’s discipline was not an abstraction for the people it was applied to,

and the claims board’s list of 11,103 names is what it looked like from the other side.

Behind this slide: 11,103 points of light, one for each name.

(Bulatlat, 2018)

An ideal authoritarian state uses coercion precisely,

but precise coercion is still coercion, and someone bears it.

If there is a claim I would defend, it is a narrow one.

Strength and cleanliness may not be opposites,

and the second may be the condition that decides whether the first is worth having.

Marcos showed that iron without an incorruptible centre rusts within a generation,

and Singapore shows that iron with one can last, at least so far.

Whether the wager can be won more than once, and by people who are not Lee, is an open and testable question rather than a settled one.

My ideal Philippines answers it by stipulation,

and the honest position is that a stipulation is exactly what that is.
